Section 2: culture shock, stereotypes, the cultural iceberg, community engagement and my first DIVE analysis.

Culture shock.

Strategies for overcoming it

Looking back at the U-curve, my honeymoon phase came before leaving rather than after arriving. I was really excited in the weeks before departure. The culture shock hit almost immediately, from day one. I got sick during my first week and slept about 48 hours straight after the flight. That's also when I realised what was waiting for me, like not seeing my family and friends until mid-January. The language barrier made daily life more complicated than I expected.

The adjustment phase felt like a big improvement, mostly because I started exploring the country and classes began, which let me make some really good friends. As for mastery, I feel properly adapted to life here. The only thing that's still hard is being far from the people I care about.

My strategies

I kept my usual habits to relax: reading, watching series, football and the gym. I call my family and close friends a lot. I try to stay open whenever someone suggests an activity (a hike, a trip to the south, a new cultural spot in Taipei) and I suspend judgement instead of comparing everything to home.

I also know about "reverse culture shock", the feeling of being out of place back home after a long time abroad. I think I'll only really understand it once I go through it in January.

Stereotypes and generalisations.

What people think, and what I actually see

French culture seen from Taiwan

What stereotypes of French people are common here?

People here probably picture the French as romantic, a bit arrogant or cold at first, big fans of wine, cheese and fashion. Also the classic one about strikes, and not being great at English despite how "refined" the country seems from outside.

What generalisations could you make from people who have met French people?

I'd guess most people who've met French tourists or students would say we're nicer than expected once you actually talk to us, even if the first impression can seem distant. Some might be surprised that plenty of French people don't speak English that well.

Taiwan seen from France

What are the French stereotypes about Taiwan?

That the cities are very clean and that people are very respectful of rules and of each other. Also the image of a very tech-advanced country, lots of scooters, and good street food.

How much truth do they contain?

The respect part is really true from what I've seen. The "clean" part, not so much, at least not visually. It's more nuanced than what people imagine back home.

What generalisations are you forming?

Respect here doesn't show through how things look, it shows through how people act. I was lost in the street a few times and random people stopped to help me without me asking, which almost never happens like that in Paris. At the same time the streets around Taipei can look run down, nothing like the postcard image. So my generalisation is that for Taiwanese people, respect is more about how you treat others than about keeping up appearances. At home, a "nice" street matters almost as much as being polite. I'm only basing this on a few months, so I don't want to pretend it applies to the whole country.

The culture iceberg.

Four hidden parts of culture, seen from France and from Taiwan

Authority

France

Relations with teachers and supervisors tend to be fairly friendly, not super formal.

Taiwan

Students are very respectful and the classroom stays quiet, yet they have a lot of freedom: leaving class whenever they want, being late. High respect and personal freedom together.

Time

France

People are generally on time, but it depends. Being a bit late is sometimes even done on purpose.

Taiwan

Really punctual for official things. The repairmen for my air conditioning arrived at 8am sharp, not a minute late.

Body language

France

Handshakes at work and bises between friends and family.

Taiwan

Less touching, it seems. But my landlady and roommates shook my hand straight away, so it's probably more nuanced.

Problem solving

France

People try on their own first. Being "débrouillard" is a valued quality.

Taiwan

I haven't seen enough yet. People seem to fix things fast and help each other out.

Community engagement.

Journaling

I picked a mix of activities rather than just one, since I'm already doing a lot of sport and social things here.

Badminton. I take lessons and play often with Taiwanese students, a good way to talk and hang out with locals outside class.

Football. We joined the school's club, train regularly, and sometimes travel on weekends to play other universities in Taipei.

Gym. Several times a week, usually with friends I've made here.

French lessons. I teach basic French to a Taiwanese friend. It's harder than expected, especially pronunciation: French has many sounds that don't exist in Mandarin. Watching them struggle made me realise how complicated my own language sounds from the outside.

Applying DIVE.

Registering for sports at Ming Chi University

Describe

To register for sports (badminton, gym, football), we had to go through one person for everything. He spoke very little English and didn't seem willing to talk to us. It took several visits, each time with a new problem with the documents. At one point he handed a document back without looking at me, just saying "End" in a dry tone.

Interpret

Maybe he isn't used to foreign students. Maybe the language barrier annoyed him as much as us. Maybe that's just how he is after a day of boring paperwork. Or this short, no-nonsense style may be normal administratively, and no local would think twice about it.

Verify

I haven't checked with Taiwanese friends or other international students yet. It would be worth asking whether they get the same treatment.

Evaluate

I shouldn't take this personally. Next time I'll stay patient: it's likely more about how admin works here, or who he is, than about us.
